# 1. Introduction to Cost Estimating

*No MCQs were asked on the Lecture 02 slides (only discussion prompts). All questions below are additional.*

### Q1.1 [Additional] Which of the following is a **job (project) overhead** cost?
- A. Rent for the contractor's main office
- B. Site supervision and site utilities
- C. Profit
- D. Salaries of the home-office accounting department

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Main-office rent is **office (general) overhead**: it supports the whole company, not one job.
- **B — Correct.** Job overhead is indirect cost tied to a specific project: site supervision, site utilities, taxes, bonds, cleanup.
- **C — Incorrect.** Profit is part of **markup**, which is added on top of total cost to get price.
- **D — Incorrect.** Home-office accounting is office overhead, the same category as A.

### Q1.2 [Additional] Which relationship correctly describes cost versus price?
- A. Price = direct cost only
- B. Total cost = direct cost + indirect cost; Price = total cost + markup
- C. Price = direct cost + markup; indirect cost is absorbed in profit
- D. Total cost = price − direct cost

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Price must also cover job overhead, office overhead and markup, or the contractor loses money.
- **B — Correct.** Direct (material, labor, equipment, subcontract) + indirect (job and office overhead) = total cost; adding markup (profit) gives price.
- **C — Incorrect.** Indirect costs are real costs, not part of profit. Dropping them overstates profit.
- **D — Incorrect.** This rearrangement has no meaning in the cost model.

### Q1.3 [Additional] Which cost component can usually be estimated with the **highest** accuracy?
- A. Material
- B. Labor
- C. Equipment
- D. All are equally accurate

**Answer: A**
- **A — Correct.** Material quantities come from the takeoff and prices from supplier quotes, so material can be estimated fairly accurately.
- **B — Incorrect.** Labor depends on productivity, which varies with weather, crew, supervision and site conditions. It is considerably harder to estimate.
- **C — Incorrect.** Equipment cost also depends on production rates and operating conditions. It is also hard to estimate accurately.
- **D — Incorrect.** The lecture specifically contrasts material (easier) with labor and equipment (harder).

### Q1.4 [Additional] Which statement about a **rough (conceptual) estimate** is TRUE?
- A. It requires complete drawings and specifications.
- B. It takes more effort than a detailed estimate but is less accurate.
- C. It is prepared during planning/design with available information, takes less time and effort, and has lower expected accuracy.
- D. It is the estimate a contractor uses to set the bid price.

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incorrect.** Complete drawings and specs are the basis of the **detailed** estimate.
- **B — Incorrect.** A rough estimate takes **less** effort, not more.
- **C — Correct.** This matches the lecture definition: approximate, early, less effort/time/detail, lower accuracy.
- **D — Incorrect.** Bid prices come from a carefully prepared **detailed** contractor's estimate.

### Q1.5 [Additional] In the AACE International Cost Estimate Classification System, what is the **primary** characteristic used to assign an estimate class?
- A. Expected accuracy range
- B. Estimating methodology
- C. Level of project definition
- D. End usage of the estimate

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incorrect.** Accuracy is a **secondary** characteristic; it generally improves as definition increases.
- **B — Incorrect.** Methodology (stochastic vs. deterministic) is secondary.
- **C — Correct.** Only the level of project definition (% of complete definition) determines the class; the others correlate with it.
- **D — Incorrect.** End usage is secondary. One party's "budget" estimate can be another's "bid" at the same class.

### Q1.6 [Additional] An AACE **Class 5** estimate is best described as:
- A. 50–100% project definition, deterministic, used as a check estimate or bid
- B. 0–2% project definition, stochastic or judgment-based, used for screening or feasibility, widest accuracy range
- C. 10–40% project definition, used for budget authorization
- D. The most expensive estimate to prepare

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** That describes **Class 1** (closest to full definition).
- **B — Correct.** Class 5 has the lowest definition, uses stochastic/judgment methods, and has the widest accuracy range (index 4–20).
- **C — Incorrect.** That is **Class 3**.
- **D — Incorrect.** Class 5 has the **lowest** preparation effort (index 1); effort rises toward Class 1.

### Q1.7 [Additional] A **deterministic** estimating method is one that:
- A. Uses statistical relationships such as cost per bed or cost per car
- B. Multiplies counted or measured quantities of each item by known unit costs
- C. Relies mainly on the estimator's judgment
- D. Is used only for Class 5 estimates

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Cost-per-function relationships are **stochastic**: the variable (beds, cars) is not a direct measure of the work.
- **B — Correct.** Deterministic = takeoff quantities × unit costs; little conjecture. Detailed estimates are deterministic.
- **C — Incorrect.** Judgment-based estimating belongs at the stochastic/Class 5 end.
- **D — Incorrect.** Deterministic methods dominate at Classes 1 and 2.

### Q1.8 [Additional] Which is **NOT** a typical purpose of an **architect's/engineer's** estimate?
- A. Checking project feasibility
- B. Evaluating design alternatives
- C. Checking contractors' bids
- D. Controlling progress on the construction site

**Answer: D**
- **A, B, C — Incorrect choices (they ARE A/E purposes).** The A/E estimate is used for feasibility, financing, evaluating design alternatives and checking bids.
- **D — Correct.** Controlling site progress (and developing the bid price) is a purpose of the **contractor's** estimate.

### Q1.9 [Additional] Under which delivery system does the owner contract with a **single entity** for design and construction, priced through a proposal rather than a competitive bid?
- A. Design-Bid-Build
- B. Design-Build
- C. Construction Management (agency)
- D. Public-Private Partnership only

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** DBB has separate contracts with the designer and the contractor, and the contractor is selected by competitive bidding.
- **B — Correct.** Design-Build: one contract with the design-builder, lump-sum proposal based on program requirements, integrated design and construction.
- **C — Incorrect.** In CM, the owner contracts with the designer and the CM, then with multiple trade contractors through competitive bids.
- **D — Incorrect.** P3s involve private financing/operation; they are not defined by a single design-build proposal.

### Q1.10 [Additional] What distinguishes **CM at Risk** from agency CM?
- A. The CM designs the project.
- B. The CM guarantees a maximum price and therefore bears cost risk.
- C. The CM is selected by low competitive bid.
- D. There is no difference.

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Design stays with the design team in both forms.
- **B — Correct.** The CM at Risk guarantees a maximum price, shifting cost risk from the owner to the CM.
- **C — Incorrect.** Selection method is not the defining difference.
- **D — Incorrect.** Risk allocation is the key difference.

### Q1.11 [Additional] The main **cost-related disadvantage** of Design-Bid-Build noted in class is:
- A. No competitive bidding
- B. Late involvement of the contractor, which limits value engineering
- C. The owner has only one contract
- D. It cannot use unit-price contracts

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** DBB *is* competitively bid; that's one of its features.
- **B — Correct.** The contractor joins after design is complete, so there's little chance to apply construction knowledge (value engineering) to the design.
- **C — Incorrect.** DBB has **two** separate contracts (designer and contractor).
- **D — Incorrect.** DBB can use lump-sum or unit-price contracts.

### Q1.12 [Additional] Under a **unit-price contract**, the contractor is finally paid:
- A. Estimated quantities × bid unit prices
- B. Actual quantities × bid unit prices
- C. The lump sum on the bid form, regardless of quantities
- D. Actual cost plus a fee

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Estimated quantities are used only to **compare bids** and set the bid total.
- **B — Correct.** Payment is based on actual measured quantities at the bid unit prices, so the final contract price changes if quantities differ.
- **C — Incorrect.** That is a lump-sum contract.
- **D — Incorrect.** That is a cost-plus-fee contract.

### Q1.13 [Additional] The key **advantage to the owner** of a lump-sum (fixed-price) contract is:
- A. Scope changes cost nothing.
- B. The owner knows the project cost before contracting.
- C. Design errors are the contractor's responsibility automatically.
- D. It allows payment by actual quantities.

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** The disadvantage of lump sum is precisely that changes in scope open the door to extra cost.
- **B — Correct.** The price is fixed for a defined scope, so the owner knows the cost up front.
- **C — Incorrect.** Errors in the documents also open the door to increased cost (owner's exposure).
- **D — Incorrect.** That describes unit-price contracts.

### Q1.14 [Additional] Which is the biggest **risk to the owner** in a cost-plus-**percentage**-fee contract?
- A. The contractor has no incentive to control cost, since the fee grows as cost grows.
- B. The contractor carries all cost risk.
- C. The price is fixed before work starts.
- D. Quantities must be measured exactly.

**Answer: A**
- **A — Correct.** A percentage fee rewards higher cost. A GMP and incentive clauses are used to counter this.
- **B — Incorrect.** In cost-plus, the **owner** carries most cost risk (costs are reimbursed).
- **C — Incorrect.** Cost-plus is used precisely when a fixed price can't be set (unclear scope, urgency).
- **D — Incorrect.** Measuring quantities matters for unit-price contracts.

### Q1.15 [Additional] According to the CII influence–expenditure diagram, the ability to influence project cost is **greatest** when:
- A. Expenditures are highest, during construction
- B. During business planning and pre-project planning, when expenditures are still low
- C. During start-up
- D. During operation of the facility

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** By execution most decisions are locked in; influence is decreasing rapidly while spending is high.
- **B — Correct.** Early phases have major influence and small expenditure; good early estimates and decisions save the most.
- **C — Incorrect.** Start-up is late in execution; influence is low.
- **D — Incorrect.** Operation has the lowest influence on capital cost.

### Q1.16 [Additional] A good estimator must estimate quantities of:
- A. Only the materials that remain in the finished structure
- B. Both permanent (contracted) materials and temporary materials such as formwork and temporary facilities
- C. Only items listed on the bid form
- D. Only labor-hours

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Ignoring temporary works (forms, shoring, temporary facilities) would badly underestimate cost.
- **B — Correct.** The estimator "builds the project virtually," including temporary materials and alternative construction methods.
- **C — Incorrect.** Bid items roll up many activities, including temporary work.
- **D — Incorrect.** Labor-hours are only one component.

# 2. Rough Estimating & Cost Indices

### Q2.1 [In class] As the size of the project increases, the cost increases at:
- A. The same rate
- B. A lower rate
- C. A higher rate

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** If cost rose at the same rate as size, unit cost ($/sf) would stay constant. In reality fixed costs (mobilization, supervision, design features) are spread over more area.
- **B — Correct.** Total cost goes up, but at a lower rate than size, so **unit cost decreases** (economies of scale). This is why the RS Means Square Foot Project Size Modifier is below 1.0 for larger-than-typical buildings.
- **C — Incorrect.** A higher rate describes **quality**: as quality increases, cost increases at a higher rate.

### Q2.2 [Additional] The weighted unit cost formula used in class is:
- A. UC = (A + B + C) / 3
- B. UC = (A + 4B + C) / 6, where A = minimum, B = average, C = maximum unit cost
- C. UC = (A + 4B + C) / 6, where B = median
- D. UC = (4A + B + 4C) / 9

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** That is a simple average of min, average and max; it is not the class formula.
- **B — Correct.** The average gets a weight of 4; min and max each get 1, divided by 6. In Ex. 1: (13,110.40 + 4 × 18,716.44 + 23,888.00) / 6 = $18,644.03/car.
- **C — Incorrect.** B is the **average** of past projects, not the median (the median is what you use from RS Means tables).
- **D — Incorrect.** This weights the extremes most heavily, the opposite of the intent.

### Q2.3 [In class – converted] Past project records show unit costs that vary mainly because the projects were different **sizes**. Which approach is most appropriate?
- A. Use the weighted unit cost formula, since it handles any variability
- B. Develop a unit cost–size relationship from the records and use it to adjust for size
- C. Use the maximum unit cost to be conservative
- D. Use the minimum unit cost to be competitive

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** The weighted unit cost is recommended when you **don't know** the reason for variability. If you know the cause (size), the formula throws that information away.
- **B — Correct.** When the cause is known, adjust for it directly (Handout #2: unit cost–size relationship). This gives a more accurate unit cost.
- **C — Incorrect.** Picking the maximum ignores the size effect and overprices the job.
- **D — Incorrect.** Picking the minimum ignores the size effect and underprices the job.

### Q2.4 [Additional] When should you use **published data** (RS Means, ENR) instead of **company records** for unit cost per function?
- A. Always; published data is always more accurate
- B. When the company has no records for previously completed projects similar to the proposed one
- C. Only for projects outside the U.S.
- D. Only when the project is very large

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Company records for similar projects reflect the company's own methods and costs and are preferred when available.
- **B — Correct.** Method 1 is company records (weighted unit cost); Method 2 is published data when such records are unavailable.
- **C — Incorrect.** Location is handled with cost indices, not by switching data sources.
- **D — Incorrect.** Size is handled by size adjustment, not by switching data sources.

### Q2.5 [In class – converted] Why does RS Means recommend using the **median** column for preliminary figures?
- A. It is always the lowest value.
- B. It represents the middle project and is not distorted by unusually cheap or expensive projects.
- C. It already includes the location adjustment.
- D. It is the average of the ¼ and ¾ columns.

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** The ¼ column is lower than the median.
- **B — Correct.** Half the projects cost less and half more; extreme projects don't pull the median the way they pull an average.
- **C — Incorrect.** Location must still be adjusted using City Cost Indexes or Location Factors.
- **D — Incorrect.** The median is not computed from the quartiles.

### Q2.6 [In class – converted] In the RS Means Square Foot Project Costs table, the **¼ column** means:
- A. One-quarter of the median cost
- B. 25% of the projects in the database had costs at or below this value
- C. The cost of the first quarter of construction
- D. The cost for a building one-quarter of typical size

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** It's a statistical quartile, not a fraction of the median.
- **B — Correct.** ¼ = lower quartile (25% of projects cost less); ¾ = upper quartile (75% cost less).
- **C — Incorrect.** The table has nothing to do with construction timing.
- **D — Incorrect.** Size effects come from the Project Size Modifier, not the quartile columns.

### Q2.7 [Additional] In Example 8, the Square Foot Project Size Modifier was **0.94**. This tells you that:
- A. The proposed building is smaller than the typical building, so unit cost goes up
- B. The proposed building is larger than the typical building, so unit cost goes down
- C. Location makes the project 6% cheaper
- D. The building is of lower quality

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** A smaller-than-typical building gets a modifier **above** 1.0 (higher unit cost).
- **B — Correct.** Larger than typical → economies of scale → cost multiplier below 1.0 ($275.93 × 0.94 = $259.37/sf).
- **C — Incorrect.** Location is applied separately (99.4/100 for Champaign).
- **D — Incorrect.** The size modifier reflects size only, not quality.

### Q2.8 [Additional] A building cost $1,000,000 in 2004 (index 143.7). The 2026 index is 307.7. Which is the correct time-adjusted cost?
- A. $1,000,000 × 143.7 / 307.7 = $467,014
- B. $1,000,000 × 307.7 / 143.7 = $2,141,267
- C. $1,000,000 × (307.7 − 143.7) / 100 = $1,640,000
- D. $1,000,000 + 307.7 − 143.7

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** The ratio is inverted. Costs rose since 2004, so the answer must be larger than $1,000,000.
- **B — Correct.** Cost(year A) = Cost(year B) × Index A / Index B, where A is the year you want.
- **C — Incorrect.** Indices are used as a ratio, not a difference.
- **D — Incorrect.** Indices are not dollar amounts to add.

### Q2.9 [Additional] Champaign has a city cost index of 99.4 and Washington, D.C. has 95.7. A project built in D.C. is being re-estimated for Champaign. The Champaign estimate will be:
- A. Lower than the D.C. cost
- B. Higher than the D.C. cost
- C. The same, since both are close to 100
- D. Impossible to find without the time index

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** A higher index means more expensive construction; Champaign's index is higher.
- **B — Correct.** $3,500,000 × 99.4 / 95.7 = $3,635,318.70.
- **C — Incorrect.** Even small index differences change the cost proportionally.
- **D — Incorrect.** If both projects are current, only location needs adjusting. (If they were in different years, you would adjust for time as well.)

### Q2.10 [Additional] Under AACEI guidelines, the **gross floor area** of a building is measured:
- A. Inside face to inside face of exterior walls, deducting stairs and elevator shafts
- B. Outside face to outside face of exterior walls (or center line of common walls), with no deduction for stairs, elevators or shafts, and including basements, mezzanines and penthouses
- C. Only the usable (rentable) area
- D. Ground floor footprint only

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Gross area uses outside faces and does **not** deduct openings.
- **B — Correct.** This is the class/AACEI rule for gross floor area.
- **C — Incorrect.** Usable/rentable area is a different (net) measure.
- **D — Incorrect.** Total gross floor area = area of one floor × number of floors, plus basements, penthouses, etc.

### Q2.11 [Additional] Two buildings have the same floor area. Building X is square; Building Y is a long rectangle. All else equal, with the unit area method:
- A. Y costs less per sf because rectangles are easier to frame
- B. X costs less per sf because it has less exterior wall per unit of floor area
- C. They cost the same per sf because floor area is equal
- D. Shape only affects unit volume estimates

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** The rectangle has more perimeter, so more costly exterior wall.
- **B — Correct.** The lecture states square buildings cost less per unit area than rectangular buildings because of less outside wall.
- **C — Incorrect.** Shape is one of the listed factors that affects unit-area costs.
- **D — Incorrect.** Shape affects unit-area estimates too (as do ceiling height and column spacing).

### Q2.12 [Additional] The **unit volume** method is most frequently used for:
- A. Single-family homes
- B. Warehouses and industrial facilities
- C. Highways
- D. Hospitals, using cost per bed

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Housing is usually estimated per unit area.
- **B — Correct.** Ceiling heights in warehouses and industrial buildings vary a lot, so volume captures cost better than area.
- **C — Incorrect.** Highways are linear projects, not volume-based buildings.
- **D — Incorrect.** Cost per bed is the unit cost per function method.

### Q2.13 [Additional] Which rough estimating method is generally the **most accurate**?
- A. Unit cost per function
- B. Unit area
- C. Parameter cost
- D. Partial takeoff

**Answer: D**
- **A — Incorrect.** Least detailed (Level 1); based on a single measure like cars or beds.
- **B — Incorrect.** Better than per-function but still one overall rate.
- **C — Incorrect.** More detailed (building systems × parameters) but still based on parameter costs.
- **D — Correct.** Partial takeoff uses quantities from partially completed design documents (e.g., all structural concrete × average unit cost). It is the most accurate preliminary estimate.

### Q2.14 [Additional] A **parameter cost estimate** works by:
- A. Multiplying total floor area by a single $/sf
- B. Multiplying appropriate units (floor area, height, perimeter, etc.) by parameter costs for each building component/system
- C. Doing a complete quantity takeoff
- D. Using cost per seat or per student

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** That's the unit area method.
- **B — Correct.** Components such as foundations, superstructure, exterior, mechanical and electrical are each priced with their own parameter (as in the AACEI parametric model).
- **C — Incorrect.** A complete takeoff is a detailed estimate.
- **D — Incorrect.** That's unit cost per function.

### Q2.15 [Additional] RS Means says the square-foot (unit area) approach should be **discontinued** when:
- A. The project is over 100,000 sf
- B. Design details become available, at which point the project should be priced by its components
- C. The location factor is below 100
- D. The median value is unavailable

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Large size is handled with the size modifier, not by abandoning the method.
- **B — Correct.** Square-foot costs are for the conceptual stage; once details exist, price the actual components.
- **C — Incorrect.** Location is just an adjustment.
- **D — Incorrect.** Not the reason given; the ¼ and ¾ columns also exist.

### Q2.16 [Additional] As project **quality** increases, cost:
- A. Decreases
- B. Increases at a lower rate
- C. Increases at a higher rate
- D. Does not change

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incorrect.** Higher quality costs more.
- **B — Incorrect.** A lower rate describes **size**, not quality.
- **C — Correct.** The lecture states cost increases at a higher rate as quality increases.
- **D — Incorrect.** Quality is a listed cost-adjustment factor.

# 3. Labor Cost (Cost Rates & Production Rates)

### Q3.1 [In class] You started a public works highway project in July 2026. What happens if the Prevailing Wage Rate changes while the project is ongoing?
- A. Prevailing wage rates should be checked monthly, and where there is a change, the revised rate applies.
- B. Apply the July 2026 Prevailing Wage Rate.

**Answer: A**
- **A — Correct.** The Illinois Department of Labor updates rates monthly; contractors must track changes and pay the revised rate going forward.
- **B — Incorrect.** Locking in the rate at project start would violate the Act if rates rise. Violators must pay the difference plus penalties.

### Q3.2 [In class] Unemployment tax (combined federal & state) rate varies:
- A. From year to year, or even from quarter to quarter
- B. From state to state
- C. According to the employment stability of the employer
- D. All the above

**Answer: D**
- **A — Partly true, but incomplete.** Rates and wage bases are set annually and the Illinois rate can change quarterly.
- **B — Partly true, but incomplete.** Each state sets its own unemployment system and rates.
- **C — Partly true, but incomplete.** Illinois uses an experience-adjusted rate: the more unemployment an employer causes, the higher its rate.
- **D — Correct.** All three are true.

### Q3.3 [In class] Which of the following statements is **not** correct?
- A. The federal unemployment tax provides benefits for employees who become unemployed.
- B. The federal unemployment tax rate can be reduced if the employer pays the state unemployment tax on a timely basis.
- C. The wage base for the federal and state unemployment taxes are the same: the first $7,000 of an employee's gross earnings for the year.
- D. None

**Answer: C**
- **A — Correct statement (not the answer).** That is FUTA's purpose.
- **B — Correct statement (not the answer).** Timely state payment earns up to a 5.4% credit: 6.0% − 5.4% = 0.6% net FUTA.
- **C — Incorrect statement (the answer).** FUTA's wage base is $7,000, but Illinois' is **$14,250** (2026).
- **D — Incorrect.** Statement C is wrong, so "None" can't be the answer.

### Q3.4 [In class] In the state of Illinois, in comparison to roofing work:
- A. A higher workers' compensation premium rate is charged for interior tile work
- B. A lower premium rate is charged for interior tile work
- C. The same premium rate is charged for interior tile work

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Roofing (falls from height) is much more dangerous than interior tile setting.
- **B — Correct.** Workers' comp premiums vary by type of work and its risk of injury. Low-risk interior work gets a lower rate.
- **C — Incorrect.** Rates are set by class code, so different trades have different rates.

### Q3.5 [In class] Production records/reports should indicate average production rates (pick the best answer):
- A. Per day (preferably) or per week, i.e., for a relatively short period
- B. Per project, i.e., for a complete project
- C. Per month, i.e., for a complete month

**Answer: A**
- **A — Correct.** Short-period records can be tied to the specific conditions that existed (weather, crew, job conditions), so they can be reused for similar future conditions.
- **B — Incorrect.** A whole-project average hides how productivity changed with conditions, so it's hard to apply to a new job.
- **C — Incorrect.** A month still averages over too many different conditions.

### Q3.6 [In class] Which activity requires more time?
- A. Fabricate and erect wood forms for concrete stairs
- B. Fabricate and erect wood forms for concrete foundation walls

**Answer: A**
- **A — Correct.** Stairs are complex: many cuts, risers, treads and odd angles. Complexity increases labor-hours per unit.
- **B — Incorrect.** Foundation walls are simple, repetitive, flat forms that go up quickly.

### Q3.7 [In class] The use of machines improves productivity:
- A. Yes
- B. No
- C. Sometimes

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incorrect.** Not always. In small areas, manual surface treatment of a concrete floor can be better than a machine.
- **B — Incorrect.** Usually machines do help, so "No" is wrong.
- **C — Correct.** Usually, but not always.

### Q3.8 [In class] The higher the crew size, the higher the productivity:
- A. Yes
- B. No
- C. Sometimes

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incorrect.** Too large a crew causes congestion and interference, which lowers productivity.
- **B — Incorrect.** Adding a worker can help (e.g., a 3-person crew erecting wood elements in a small house vs. 2).
- **C — Correct.** It depends; there is an optimal crew size.

### Q3.9 [In class – converted] The employer must remit to the federal government ___% of each employee's first $184,500 (Social Security + Medicare), and the employer's own contribution is ___%.
- A. 7.65%; 7.65%
- B. 15.3%; 7.65%
- C. 12.4%; 6.2%
- D. 15.3%; 15.3%

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** 7.65% is only the employer's share. The employer also withholds and remits the employee's 7.65%.

### Q4.6 [Additional] For a rubber-tired machine, why is the tire cost subtracted when computing depreciation?

# 5. Earthwork & Excavation

### Q5.1 [In class] In which state does the soil have the **highest volume**?
- A. Bank (in-place)
- B. Loose
- C. Compacted
- D. Volume does not change

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Bank is the reference volume; excavation loosens the soil and adds voids.
- **B — Correct.** Loose = after excavation; it swells (L = (1 + Sw/100) × B).
- **C — Incorrect.** Compaction removes voids, giving the **smallest** volume (C = (1 − Sh/100) × B).
- **D — Incorrect.** Soil volume changes during operations, which is why the units BCY/LCY/CCY exist.

### Q5.2 [In class] In which state does the soil have the **highest unit weight**?
- A. Bank (in-place)
- B. Loose
- C. Compacted
- D. Unit weight does not change

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incorrect.** Bank is denser than loose but less dense than compacted.
- **B — Incorrect.** Loose has the most voids, so the **lowest** unit weight.
- **C — Correct.** Same weight of soil in the smallest volume gives the highest unit weight.
- **D — Incorrect.** Weight stays the same, but volume changes, so unit weight changes.

### Q5.3 [In class] Why is soil volume information important to estimators?

- B. To correctly convert between the three units of measure: bank, loose, compacted

- **A — Partly true.** Truck and bucket capacities are loose volumes, while the excavation quantity is usually bank.
- **B — Partly true.** The contractor may be paid in any of the three units, and the bid must match the unit in the instructions to bidders.
- **C — Correct.** Both are reasons.

### Q5.4 [In class] You are excavating a basement. Generally, the work surface is flat and stable. Which equipment would you select?

- **C — Correct.** A backhoe digs **below grade** (trenches, basements) with positive digging action and precise control. A crawler gives a stable digging platform, and a basement job needs little travel.
- **D — Incorrect.** The backhoe is right, but wheel mounting's advantages (speed and mobility **between** sites) don't help on a single basement dig, and wheels give a less stable platform for digging.

- **C — Incorrect.** It's usually corrected, not rejected.
- **D — Incorrect.** The rule follows the unit prices, not whichever total is lower.

### Q7.12 [Additional] Where can a contractor's **contingency** appear in the estimate?

# 8. Bid Unbalancing *(asked in class; likely beyond Exam 1 per the coverage sheet)*

### Q8.1 [In class] How do you unbalance a bid to **maximize early income**?
- A. Raise bid prices of later items, decrease bid prices of earlier items, and keep the same total bid price
- B. Raise bid prices of earlier items, decrease bid prices of later items, and keep the same total bid price
- C. A or B are possible ways

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** This delays income, the opposite of the goal.
- **B — Correct.** Front-loading the early items (excavation, piling, footings) brings money in sooner and improves cash flow. Start decreasing from the latest item.
- **C — Incorrect.** Only B achieves early income.

### Q8.2 [In class] Bid unbalancing to maximize early income applies to:
- A. Lump-sum contracts
- B. Unit-price contracts
- C. Both

**Answer: C**
- **A — Incomplete.** In a lump-sum contract, progress payments are based on a breakdown of the price by work item, which can be front-loaded.
- **B — Incomplete.** In a unit-price contract, raising unit prices of early items also front-loads payment.
- **C — Correct.** Early income depends on **when** work is paid, not on quantity changes, so it works under both contract types.

### Q8.3 [In class] How do you unbalance a bid to **maximize profit**?
- A. Raise bid prices of expected quantity overrun items, decrease bid prices of expected quantity underrun items, and keep the same total bid price
- B. Raise bid prices of expected underrun items, decrease bid prices of expected overrun items, and keep the same total bid price
- C. A or B are possible ways

**Answer: A**
- **A — Correct.** If actual quantities exceed estimates on items with higher unit prices, the contractor is paid more (Ex 4: piling 2,240 → 2,700 lf gives a higher actual total). If no underrun items exist, decrease later items.
- **B — Incorrect.** This would cut revenue on items that grow.
- **C — Incorrect.** Only A increases profit.

### Q8.4 [In class] Bid unbalancing to maximize profit applies to:
- A. Lump-sum contracts
- B. Unit-price contracts
- C. Both

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Lump-sum pays a fixed price no matter the quantities, so overruns don't increase payment.
- **B — Correct.** It works only when payment is based on **actual quantities × unit prices**.
- **C — Incorrect.** It doesn't apply to lump sum.

### Q8.5 [Additional] When unbalancing, the unbalanced **unit price** is calculated by dividing the unbalanced total item price by:
- A. The actual quantity expected
- B. The estimated quantity on the bid form
- C. The original unit price
- D. The factor

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Even for profit unbalancing, unit price = unbalanced total ÷ **estimated** quantity. Actual quantities are used only to forecast the result.
- **B — Correct.** Then recompute totals and adjust for rounding so the bid total stays exactly the same.
- **C — Incorrect.** Meaningless.
- **D — Incorrect.** The factor is for the balanced recap.

### Q8.6 [Additional] What is the defining constraint in all bid unbalancing?
- A. Every unit price must rise.
- B. The **total bid amount stays the same**; only the distribution among items changes.
- C. Only lump-sum items can change.
- D. Unit prices must equal direct cost.

**Answer: B**
- **A — Incorrect.** Some prices must fall to offset the increases.
- **B — Correct.** This is the definition from class. Rounding adjustments are made to hold the total exactly.
- **C — Incorrect.** Any items can change.
- **D — Incorrect.** Unbalanced prices intentionally depart from cost-based prices.
